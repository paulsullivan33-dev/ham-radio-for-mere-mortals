# Callsigns

Your **callsign** is your name on the air. The FCC assigns one to
every licensed amateur — a short string of letters and numbers,
unique worldwide. You say it every time you identify, and other
hams use it to look you up, log contacts with you, and send you
confirmation cards.

US callsigns start with **K**, **N**, **W**, or **AA–AL**. A
typical Technician callsign looks like **KI5ABC** — two or three
letters, a single digit, then one to three letters. The digit
roughly indicates your region of the country (5 covers Texas and
the south-central US).

## The identification rule

FCC rule **§97.119** requires you to identify with your callsign:

- **At the end of each contact**, and
- **At least every 10 minutes** during a contact.

That's it — no ID needed at the start, no ID every time you key
the mic. A common pattern on a repeater: chat for a while, then
sign off with your callsign. Long ragchews just need the callsign
dropped in every ten minutes ("...anyway, KI5ABC, back to you").

Identify in English (or with the international phonetic alphabet),
in a mode the other station can understand — voice, Morse, or an
approved digital ID.

## The phonetic alphabet

Radio channels are noisy. "B," "D," "E," "G," and "T" all sound
alike through static, so hams spell callsigns with the
**NATO/ICAO phonetic alphabet** — one distinct word per letter.
Learn it; you'll use it constantly.

| A Alfa | B Bravo | C Charlie | D Delta | E Echo |
| F Foxtrot | G Golf | H Hotel | I India | J Juliett |
| K Kilo | L Lima | M Mike | N November | O Oscar |
| P Papa | Q Quebec | R Romeo | S Sierra | T Tango |
| U Uniform | V Victor | W Whiskey | X Xray | Y Yankee | Z Zulu |

Digits are spoken normally: "five," "niner" for 9 (aviation
habit many hams keep — "niner" is unmistakable).

Note the official spellings: **Alfa** (not Alpha), **Juliett**
(not Juliet), **Xray** (not X-ray). These are the ICAO standard
forms. You'll hear variants on the air — "America" for A,
"Japan" for J — and nobody will correct you, but the standard
words exist because they're the least ambiguous. Use them.

So **KI5ABC** becomes *"Kilo India Five Alfa Bravo Charlie."*

## Why it matters

On a noisy channel, the phonetic alphabet is the difference
between a logged contact and a garbled one. Contest stations and
DX pileups (dozens of stations calling one rare station) run
entirely on phonetics — clear, fast, standard. It's also just
courtesy: spelling your callsign phonetically the first time
saves everyone a round of "was that B or D?"
