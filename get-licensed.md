# Getting Licensed

In the US, transmitting on amateur frequencies requires an FCC
license. The entry-level license is **Technician**. Getting it
means passing one written exam — no Morse code, no equipment
required, no prior experience.

There are three US license classes, in order: **Technician**,
**General**, **Extra**. Each unlocks more frequencies. Technician
is where everyone starts, and it's plenty: local and regional
talking on VHF/UHF, plus a useful slice of worldwide shortwave
(see [Bands and Modes](bands-and-modes.md)). You can upgrade later
without re-taking anything you've already passed.

## The exam

- **Element 2** — the Technician written exam.
- **35 multiple-choice questions**, drawn from a public question
  pool. You need **26 correct (74%) to pass**.
- The current pool is the **NCVEC 2026–2030 Technician pool**:
  409 questions, effective **July 1, 2026 through June 30, 2030**.
  Every exam question comes word-for-word from this public pool —
  there are no surprise questions.
- **No Morse code test.** The FCC eliminated Morse proficiency
  testing in 2007 (WT Docket 05-235).

## The path, step by step

### 1. Get an FRN first

Before exam day, register with the FCC's CORES system to get an
**FRN** (FCC Registration Number) — a ten-digit ID that stands in
for your Social Security number on all FCC paperwork. Bring it to
the exam session. You cannot complete licensing without one.

### 2. Study

The whole pool is public, so the honest study method is: learn the
material, then drill practice exams until you consistently pass.

- **hamstudy.org** — free flashcards and practice exams built on
  the current pool. This is the single most-recommended study tool
  in the hobby, and it's free.
- **ARRL license manuals** — the ARRL (the national amateur radio
  association) publishes study guides for each class. Good if you
  want explanations, not just answers.
- **Practice exams** — take full 35-question practice exams until
  you're scoring comfortably above 26. A typical rule of thumb:
  pass five in a row before you book a session.

The exam covers FCC rules, operating procedures, basic
electricity and electronics, antennas, and safety. You don't need
to be an engineer — the math is arithmetic (Ohm's law, power
calculations), and the rules section is memorization.

### 3. Find an exam session

Exams are given by **Volunteer Examiners (VEs)** — licensed hams
accredited by a **VEC** (Volunteer Examiner Coordinator). Sessions
run all over the country, in person and online.

- The ARRL keeps a searchable list of exam sessions on its
  website (search "ARRL find an exam session").
- **In person:** typical format is a paper or computer exam at a
  club meeting, library, or hamfest. Bring photo ID, your FRN,
  and the session fee.
- **Online:** remote sessions via video call exist (webcam,
  quiet room, ID check). Availability varies by VEC — the big
  remote teams publish their schedules online.

### 4. Pay the fees

Two separate fees, paid to two different places:

| Fee | Amount | Paid to |
|---|---|---|
| Exam session fee | Typically **$14–15**; some sessions (e.g., Laurel VEC teams) are **free** | The VEC team at the session |
| FCC application fee | **$35** | The FCC, via CORES, *after* you pass |

The $35 FCC fee applies to new license applications. You do **not**
pay it at the exam — after the VEC files your passing result, the
FCC emails you payment instructions, and you pay through your
CORES account. Upgrades to a higher class later are exempt from
the FCC fee (you'd only pay the session fee again).

### 5. Get your callsign

Once the VEC files your result and you've paid the $35 FCC fee,
the FCC issues your license and **callsign** — typically within a
few days. It appears in the FCC's **ULS** (Universal Licensing
System) database, and that's the moment you're legal to transmit.
Your paper license follows electronically.

## After you're licensed

- Your license is valid for **10 years**, renewable without
  retesting.
- Your callsign is yours to keep through renewals (you can also
  request a different one through the vanity callsign program).
- Upgrading to General means passing one more 35-question exam
  (Element 3); Extra adds a 50-question exam (Element 4). Many
  hams stay happily at Technician for years.

> **Check:** Before booking a session, verify the current pool
> dates. The pool in use from July 1, 2026 through June 30, 2030
> is the 2026–2030 Technician pool — make sure your study materials
> match it. Materials written for the old 2022–2026 pool will
> mislead you.
