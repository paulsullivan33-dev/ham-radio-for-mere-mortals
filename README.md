# Ham Radio for Mere Mortals

**Ham radio** is a licensed hobby where ordinary people talk to each
other — and experiment — using radio. No subscription, no cell tower,
no internet required. People use it to chat across town, bounce
signals off the atmosphere to reach other continents, send data on
weak signals a computer can decode, and help out when storms knock
out normal communications.

This guide takes you from "sounds interesting" to licensed and making
your first contact. No electronics background assumed.

## What this guide covers

- **What ham radio is** — and what it isn't (not CB, not a
  podcast, not a phone replacement)
- **Getting licensed** — the Technician exam path, costs, and what
  to study
- **Callsigns** — your on-air identity and the phonetic alphabet
- **Bands and modes** — HF/VHF/UHF in plain language, and what a
  Technician can actually use
- **Repeaters** — the trick that lets a handheld reach across a
  city
- **Your first radio** — handheld vs. mobile vs. base, and what to
  look for (no brand picks, no prices)
- **Your first contact** — exactly what to say on a repeater
- **Antennas** — SWR, simple wire antennas, and safety
- **Digital modes** — FT8 and computer-assisted contacts
- **Emergency communications** — ARES, RACES, and the rules
- **Beyond talking** — satellites, POTA, SOTA
- **Troubleshooting** — symptom → fix for the common beginner
  problems

## What it doesn't cover

- Electronics theory or building your own radios (the *ideas* you
  need are here; circuit design is its own hobby)
- Morse code operation (the FCC dropped the Morse requirement in
  2007, so it's optional — appreciated by enthusiasts, not taught
  here)
- Commercial two-way radio (GMRS, FRS, business band) — related,
  different rules
- Antenna engineering math

## A note on countries

**This guide is US-focused.** In the United States, amateur radio
is regulated by the FCC (Federal Communications Commission):
licensing classes, exam structure, band limits, and fees below are
all American. Licensing exists in nearly every country, but the
classes, callsign formats, and rules differ — if you're outside the
US, your national regulator and local club are the authority.

## How to use this guide

Each chapter is one idea, written to be read in any order — but the
first six follow the path most people take: understand → license →
get on the air. **Check:** blocks show something you can verify
yourself. **Safety:** marks the things that can genuinely hurt you
or your gear.
