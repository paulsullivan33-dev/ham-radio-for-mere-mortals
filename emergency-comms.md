# Emergency Communications

When hurricanes, tornadoes, wildfires, or ice storms take down
cell towers and power lines, amateur radio keeps working. Hams
have a long, genuine record of passing emergency traffic —
and the hobby maintains organized programs for exactly this.
This chapter covers what those programs are and the rules that
govern them.

## ARES and RACES

Two organizations, often confused, both worth knowing:

- **ARES** — the **Amateur Radio Emergency Service**. Organized
  by the ARRL, staffed by volunteer hams. ARES groups train
  with local served agencies (emergency management, Red Cross,
  hospitals) and activate for drills and real events. Joining
  your local ARES group is the most direct way to do emergency
  communications as a ham.
- **RACES** — the **Radio Amateur Civil Emergency Service**. A
  service defined in FCC rules (47 CFR §97.407), administered
  through state and local emergency-management agencies. RACES
  stations operate only when activated by a civil-defense
  authority, and only with registered RACES operators. In
  practice, many hams hold both ARES and RACES memberships and
  serve whichever is activated.

The practical difference: ARES is the ARRL's volunteer program
and can serve any agency; RACES is the government's program and
activates under official direction. Your local emergency
coordinator can tell you which matters in your county.

## The three rules that always apply

Emergency or not, these FCC rules bind every transmission:

1. **Identify.** Callsign at the end of each contact and every
   10 minutes — §97.119. Emergencies don't suspend this.
2. **No pecuniary interest.** §97.113(a) prohibits
   communications in which you or your employer have a
   financial interest, and communications on behalf of an
   employer. You volunteer your *time and equipment*; you don't
   do your employer's business over ham radio, emergency or
   otherwise.
3. **No broadcasting.** §97.113(b) — one-way transmissions to
   the public aren't amateur radio. Emergency nets are
   directed, two-way operations, which is fine.

## How hams actually help

The realistic picture, not the movie version:

- **Health-and-welfare traffic.** "I'm okay" messages out of a
  disaster zone when phones are down — historically the bulk of
  ham emergency work.
- **Served-agency support.** Staffing a shelter, a hospital, or
  an emergency-operations center with operators who can reach
  the net when nothing else can.
- **Skywarn.** Hams as trained storm spotters, reporting
  ground truth to the National Weather Service during severe
  weather. Your local National Weather Service office runs the
  training — it's free and open to anyone.
- **Drills.** Weekly nets and annual exercises (like Field Day
  and Simulated Emergency Tests) keep skills fresh. The hams
  who help in real emergencies are the ones who practiced.

What hams *don't* do: self-deploy to disaster scenes, freelance
outside the net structure, or treat a handheld as a rescue tool.
Emergency communication is a team discipline — the net
control station directs, everyone else follows.

## Getting started

1. Join your local ARES group (ask at a club meeting or on a
   repeater net).
2. Take the free Skywarn spotter training.
3. Check into the weekly emergency or traffic net. Regular
   check-ins are the entire foundation — when something real
   happens, the net already knows your voice and your
   capabilities.

> **Check:** Find out whether your county has an ARES group and
> when its net meets. One check-in teaches you more about
> emergency procedure than any chapter can.
