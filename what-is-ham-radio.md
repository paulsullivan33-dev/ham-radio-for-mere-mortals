# What Is Ham Radio?

Ham radio (formally the **Amateur Radio Service**) is a licensed
hobby in which individuals use radio frequencies to communicate with
each other and experiment with radio technology. The license is free
of commercial purpose: you're not selling anything, you're not
broadcasting a show, you're talking to other licensed people — or
listening, which needs no license at all.

Think of it as two hobbies sharing one license:

1. **Talking to people by radio** — across town on a handheld,
   across the country on a wire antenna, or around the world when
   the atmosphere cooperates.
2. **Experimenting with radio itself** — building antennas, bouncing
   signals off satellites, sending data through the noise with
   computer help.

Most hams do some of each.

## What it is

- **Two-way communication.** You talk *and* listen. Every contact
  is a conversation (or at least an exchange), not a broadcast.
- **A licensed service.** The FCC issues you a callsign — your
  unique on-air identity. Passing a written exam is the entry fee,
  and it's a modest one (see [Getting Licensed](get-licensed.md)).
- **A technical hobby with a social layer.** The license exam covers
  rules, safety, and basic electronics, but most of the hobby is
  just... talking. Nets, clubs, contests, emergency drills.
- **Infrastructure-independent.** A handheld radio and a repeater
  on a hilltop need no internet, no cell network, no power grid
  beyond a battery. That's why hams show up after hurricanes.

## What it isn't

| Common confusion | The reality |
|---|---|
| **CB radio** | CB (Citizens Band) is unlicensed, limited to 40 channels at 27 MHz, and legally capped at 4 watts. Ham radio is licensed, spans dozens of bands, and allows up to 1500 watts (most hams use far less). Different service, different rules. |
| **Broadcasting** | Broadcasting means one station transmitting *to* an audience — AM/FM radio, podcasts. Hams are explicitly forbidden from broadcasting (47 CFR §97.113(b)). You talk *with* people, not *at* them. |
| **A phone replacement** | You can't call a phone number from a ham radio (regular traffic isn't connected to the phone network, and business content is prohibited). It's a hobby, not a telecom service. |
| **Military or police radio** | Those are separate services with their own rules. Ham radio is civilian, open, and non-commercial. |
| **Free internet** | Some hams run data networks (packet radio, mesh), but these are hobby experiments, not an ISP substitute. |

## Why people do it

Ask ten hams and you'll get ten answers, but the recurring ones:

- **The magic of distance.** Your first contact with another
  continent on a wire you hung from a tree does something to you.
- **Self-reliance.** When the grid goes down, hams still talk.
  Emergency communications (Chapter 10) is a genuine public
  service.
- **Tinkering.** Antennas, radios, digital modes, satellites —
  there's always another layer.
- **Community.** Clubs, weekly nets, field days. The hobby skews
  friendly; strangers will happily help you set up your first
  station.

## What you need to start

Less than most people assume:

1. **A license** — pass the Technician exam ([Chapter
   2](get-licensed.md)).
2. **A radio** — many beginners start with a handheld that costs
   less than the exam fees ([Chapter 6](first-radio.md)).
3. **Someone to talk to** — a local repeater or club net
   ([Chapter 5](repeaters.md)).

That's it. No tower, no thousand-dollar station, no engineering
degree.

> **Check:** You don't need a license to *listen*. A cheap scanner
> or a web-based receiver lets you hear local repeater traffic and
> shortwave broadcasts right now. Listening first is also the best
> way to learn on-air etiquette before you transmit.
