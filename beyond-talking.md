# Beyond Talking

Once you're comfortable on the repeaters, the hobby opens up in
directions that surprise most beginners. Three of the most
popular:

## Amateur satellites

Dozens of amateur satellites orbit overhead, carrying FM
repeaters and digital transponders. Working one is startlingly
accessible: a handheld radio, a handheld directional antenna,
and a satellite-tracking app. You listen for the pass, point
the antenna, and make contacts through a repeater *in space*
during the few minutes it's above your horizon.

The International Space Station carries amateur gear too —
astronauts occasionally make voice contacts, and the ISS
digipeater relays packet traffic. Hearing your signal come
back from orbit never quite gets old.

## POTA — Parks on the Air

**Parks on the Air** (POTA) turns parks into contact
opportunities: operators ("activators") set up portable
stations in parks and make contacts; home stations ("hunters")
try to work them. Thousands of parks are registered. An
activation needs a portable radio, a wire antenna in a tree,
and a battery — the barrier is a day trip, not an expedition.

POTA is arguably the friendliest on-ramp to HF operating: the
exchange is short and structured, activators *want* your
contact, and every park is a small adventure.

## SOTA — Summits on the Air

**Summits on the Air** is POTA's mountain-dwelling cousin:
activate from a registered summit, with points scaled by
elevation. It combines hiking with radio — the antenna goes up
on the summit, the contacts roll in, and you hike back down.
Chasers work activators from home.

Both programs log contacts online, award certificates, and run
entirely on volunteer organization. They're also the best
answer to "but I don't have a tower": a wire in a tree at a
park outperforms most home stations.

> **Check:** Visit the POTA website and search for registered
> parks near you — most people are surprised how many there
> are. You can "hunt" activators with nothing but your
> existing station before ever activating one yourself.
