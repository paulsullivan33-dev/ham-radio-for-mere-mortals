# Bands and Modes

Radio frequencies are divided into **bands** — ranges of
frequencies set aside for amateur use. Different bands behave
differently: some carry across town, some across the planet. The
way your voice (or data) is encoded onto the radio wave is the
**mode**.

## The bands, in plain language

Frequencies are measured in **MHz** (megahertz — millions of
cycles per second). Hams name bands by wavelength: the 2-meter
band is around 144 MHz, because the radio wave there is about 2
meters long. Lower frequency = longer wavelength = generally
farther reach, with more dependence on atmospheric conditions.

The main amateur bands (US allocations):

| Band | Frequency range | Character |
|---|---|---|
| 6 m | 50–54 MHz | "Magic band" — usually local, occasionally worldwide when conditions align |
| 2 m | 144–148 MHz | The workhorse: repeaters, local chat, emergency nets |
| 70 cm | 420–450 MHz | Like 2 m but shorter range; great for cities, handhelds |
| 10 m | 28.0–29.7 MHz | Worldwide when the sun cooperates; dead quiet otherwise |
| 20 m | 14.0–14.35 MHz | The classic long-distance band |
| 40 m | 7.0–7.3 MHz | Reliable day and night, regional to worldwide |
| 80 m | 3.5–4.0 MHz | Nighttime regional workhorse |

**VHF** (Very High Frequency, 30–300 MHz) and **UHF** (Ultra High
Frequency, 300–3000 MHz) are essentially line-of-sight: your
signal goes as far as the horizon, plus a bit. That's why
repeaters on tall towers matter so much (Chapter 5). **HF** (High
Frequency, 3–30 MHz) can bounce off the ionosphere and travel
thousands of miles — when conditions allow.

## Modes: SSB vs. FM

- **FM** (Frequency Modulation) — the same idea as FM broadcast
  radio. Clear, noise-resistant, easy. This is what nearly all
  VHF/UHF repeater and handheld talking uses.
- **SSB** (Single Sideband) — a more efficient voice mode that
  squeezes more distance out of less power. Standard for HF
  voice. Sounds a bit like Donald Duck if your receiver isn't
  tuned right — that's normal, just adjust.
- **CW** — Morse code. Still the most efficient human mode of
  all, and beloved by enthusiasts. Optional since 2007.
- **Digital modes** — computers encoding data as audio tones
  (Chapter 10 covers FT8).

Beginners live in FM on 2 m and 70 cm. It's simple, social, and
works with the cheapest radios.

## What a Technician can use

A Technician license gives you **all amateur privileges above
30 MHz** — that's 6 m, 2 m, 70 cm, and everything higher, with
full power. Plus **limited HF privileges**:

| Band | Technician slice | Allowed modes |
|---|---|---|
| 10 m | 28.000–28.300 MHz | CW, RTTY, data |
| 10 m | 28.300–28.500 MHz | CW and SSB voice |
| 15 m | 21.025–21.200 MHz | CW only |
| 40 m | 7.025–7.125 MHz | CW only |
| 80 m | 3.525–3.600 MHz | CW only |

That 10-meter SSB slice (28.3–28.5 MHz) is the Technician's
taste of worldwide voice: when solar conditions are good, 10 m
opens up and a modest station talks across continents.

## Power limits

The general amateur ceiling is **1500 watts PEP** (peak envelope
power). Technicians are limited to **200 watts PEP on HF**
(47 CFR §97.313(b)) — still far more than you'll ever need
starting out. Most handhelds run 5 watts; most beginners never
exceed 50.

The honest truth about power: on VHF/UHF, **antenna height beats
wattage** every time. Doubling your power gains you a barely
noticeable improvement; raising your antenna ten feet can double
your range.
