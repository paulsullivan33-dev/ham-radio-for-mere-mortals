# Digital Modes

**Digital modes** let a computer do the talking: your radio
sends audio tones generated by software, and software on the
other end decodes them. The standout is **FT8** — a mode so
good at pulling signals out of noise that contacts happen with
a fraction of the power voice needs, on bands that sound dead
to the ear.

## Why FT8 matters

FT8 exchanges are minimal: callsigns, signal reports, and a
sign-off, in 15-second cycles, highly structured. What it gives
up in conversation it returns in reach:

- **Weak-signal performance.** FT8 decodes signals far below
  what your ear can hear — down around −20 dB signal-to-noise.
  Bands that sound empty on SSB are full of FT8.
- **Low power friendly.** 20–30 watts on HF does worldwide
  work that would take much more on voice.
- **Modest antennas work.** The mode's efficiency compensates
  for compromise antennas — good news for beginners and
  apartment dwellers.
- **It's honest about conditions.** Watching the band fill with
  decodes tells you exactly which parts of the world are
  reachable right now.

The trade: it's not a conversation. An FT8 contact is a handshake,
not a chat. Many hams do both — FT8 for the thrill of distance,
voice for the human part.

## What you need

- A radio capable of SSB (any HF radio, or an HF-capable base
  station — see [Your First Radio](first-radio.md)).
- A computer running **WSJT-X** (free, open-source software by
  Joe Taylor, K1JT, and Steve Franke, K9AN — the "JT" in the
  name).
- An audio interface between computer and radio. Many modern
  radios have USB audio built in; older ones need a small
  external interface box.

Setup details vary by radio — your radio's manual and the
WSJT-X user guide cover the wiring. The key settings: correct
audio levels (too much audio distorts and splatters; the goal
is clean, modest drive) and accurate computer clock (FT8's
15-second cycles require your clock within a second or two —
use internet time sync).

## Where to find FT8

FT8 activity concentrates on agreed calling frequencies. Set
your radio to USB and dial in:

| Band | Frequency (MHz) |
|---|---|
| 40 m | 7.074 |
| 20 m | 14.074 |
| 15 m | 21.074 |
| 10 m | 28.074 |
| 6 m | 50.313 |
| 2 m | 144.174 |

These are the WSJT-X defaults — the watering holes. Tune to
14.074 MHz USB on any afternoon and you'll likely see decodes
within a minute.

## Beyond FT8

The WSJT-X family includes slower, even weaker-signal modes
(**WSPR** for propagation beacons — "can my 1-watt signal reach
Japan?"), and the hobby has many others: **RTTY** (radioteletype,
the old-timer), **PSK31** (keyboard chat), **packet/APRS**
(position reporting and messaging on VHF). FT8 is the on-ramp;
the rest is there when curiosity strikes.

> **Check:** Tune to 14.074 MHz in USB mode and just listen —
> you'll hear the distinctive 15-second warbling cycles. Then
> install WSJT-X, connect your radio's audio, and watch the
> decode window fill. Your first decoded callsign from another
> continent is a genuine thrill.
